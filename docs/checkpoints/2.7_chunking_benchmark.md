# Checkpoint 2.7 — The Chunking Benchmark

## What we built
- A complete head-to-head chunking benchmark: 6 strategies, same corpus, same gold set, ranked output
- Automatic `RESULTS.md` update with the benchmark table
- Clean corpus via `make_corpus.py` (no PowerShell artifacts)
- Per-chunker timing (chunk time + index time) as a third dimension alongside recall

## Benchmark results

**Corpus:** 2478 chars, 10 paragraphs (one per RAG concept)
**Gold set:** 10 queries, one per paragraph
**Embedder:** BGE-small-en-v1.5
**Index:** FAISS flat

| Rank | Chunker | recall@1 | recall@3 | MRR | chunks | avg_size | total_ms |
|------|---------|----------|----------|-----|--------|----------|----------|
| 1 | parent_child | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 10 | 279 | 113.6 |
| 2 | adaptive | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 10 | 291 | 115.9 |
| 3 | metadata_aware | 1.000 | 1.000 | 1.000 | 6 | 454 | 118.9 |
| 4 | overlapping | 0.900 | 1.000 | 0.950 | 6 | 454 | 461.0 |
| 5 | recursive | 0.900 | 1.000 | 0.950 | 8 | 353 | 139.2 |
| 6 | semantic | 0.800 | 0.900 | 0.850 | 5 | 493 | 315.9 |

## Core concepts

### 1. Empirical evidence > algorithmic sophistication
The most sophisticated chunker (semantic) ranked last. The simplest adequate solution (parent_child — just two recursive splits at different scales) ranked first. This is a direct refutation of the intuition "more complex = better."

**What actually predicted performance:** chunk size. parent_child (279 avg) and adaptive (291 avg) produced the smallest chunks and the highest precision on per-paragraph queries. The gold set had one correct paragraph per query — tight embeddings of small chunks beat diluted embeddings of large chunks.

### 2. Why semantic ranked last — the "dilution" failure mode
Semantic chunking merged "dense retrieval" and "hybrid retrieval" into one chunk (they're semantically adjacent — BGE judged them similar). The gold set has separate queries for each. The merged chunk's embedding tries to represent both topics simultaneously — its cosine similarity with "What is hybrid retrieval?" is lower than a dedicated hybrid-only chunk's would be.

**Lesson:** Semantic chunking is best when adjacent paragraphs genuinely benefit from staying together (narrative prose, meeting notes). It's worst when each paragraph is a discrete reference entry that should stay distinct (technical glossaries, API docs, RAG primers).

### 3. Overlapping is the slowest despite being "simplest"
```
overlapping: 461ms
parent_child: 113ms
```

The 4x slowdown is not in the chunking step (`t_chunk_ms`) — it's in the index step (`t_index_ms`). Overlapping chunks are large (454 avg chars). More chars = more tokens per BGE embedding = more compute per embedding call. Chunk *size* drives embedding cost, not chunk *count*.

Practical implication: on a 1M-doc corpus, overlapping chunking is significantly more expensive than parent_child despite identical algorithm complexity.

### 4. Three chunkers tied at perfect — what differentiates them?
parent_child, adaptive, and metadata_aware all hit r@1=1.000 on this corpus. How to choose?

**Generation quality (faithfulness, answer completeness):** parent_child has a context multiplier — the LLM sees 3x more context. This would show up in a faithfulness benchmark (M8) but is invisible in recall@1.

**Corpus type:**
- metadata_aware is best when your corpus has rich structure (DOCX, MD with headings, PDFs with tables)
- adaptive is best when your corpus has mixed density (code + prose, formulas + narrative)
- parent_child is a safe default for most prose corpora

**Production cost:** metadata_aware produces 6 chunks vs parent_child's 10 — fewer embeddings, smaller index, cheaper.

### 5. Data hygiene can wreck benchmarks
The PowerShell `Set-Content` command leaked into the corpus in previous tests, corrupting the first chunk of every chunker. This would have made the results unreproducible and difficult to compare across runs. `make_corpus.py` writes the corpus via Python string — clean, platform-independent, version-controllable.

**General lesson:** corpus creation is part of the benchmark pipeline, not a one-time setup step. The benchmark script should be able to recreate the corpus from scratch, guaranteeing reproducibility.

### 6. Timing as a first-class dimension
Recall is not the only cost. On a 1M-doc corpus:
- 461ms per doc (overlapping) = 128 hours to index
- 113ms per doc (parent_child) = 31 hours to index

That's a 4x difference in ingestion time — a real business constraint. A benchmark that only measures recall misses the engineering reality of production deployment.

### 7. Benchmark infrastructure is the real asset
The benchmark script takes ~5 minutes to run on your corpus. On a client's corpus, it takes 5 minutes plus embedding time. The specific numbers (parent_child wins at r@1=1.000) will change. The infrastructure will not.

When a new chunking strategy emerges (from a paper, from a hackathon, from your own experiments), adding it to the benchmark is: implement the class, add one line to `CHUNKERS`. The rest is automatic.

## Why the design choices
- **GOLD_SET reuse from M0**: same queries as the retrieval baseline — changes in recall@1 are directly comparable to M0 numbers
- **Per-chunker fresh FAISS index**: ensures no cross-contamination between strategies
- **`RESULTS.md` append**: running the benchmark multiple times (e.g., on different corpora) accumulates a history rather than overwriting
- **Ranked by (recall@1, mrr)**: MRR is the tiebreaker — among strategies with identical recall@1, the one that ranks the correct chunk higher is more useful for generation

## Interview flashcards

1. **Q:** Your most sophisticated chunker ranked last. What does that tell you?
   **A:** That algorithm sophistication is not the same as fitness for purpose. Semantic chunking is excellent for narrative prose and poor for technical reference material. The corpus type determines the right strategy. This is why you run benchmarks instead of picking the "best" algorithm from a blog post.

2. **Q:** Three chunkers tied at perfect recall. How do you choose between them?
   **A:** Run a generation-quality benchmark (faithfulness, answer completeness). parent_child has a 3x context multiplier that would show up there but is invisible in recall@1. Also consider corpus type (metadata_aware for structured docs, adaptive for mixed density) and production cost (6 chunks vs 10 for the same document).

3. **Q:** Why is overlapping 4x slower than parent_child despite being algorithmically simpler?
   **A:** Chunk size drives embedding cost, not algorithm complexity. Overlapping produces larger chunks (454 avg chars vs 279 for parent_child). More chars = more tokens per BGE call = more compute per embedding. On a 1M-doc corpus, this is a 128 vs 31 hour difference in ingestion time.

4. **Q:** How would you extend this benchmark to a client's 10GB corpus?
   **A:** Sample 1000 representative documents, run the benchmark on those. Full corpus benchmarking would take hours; a sample gives directionally correct results in minutes. Then verify on the full corpus once you've chosen a strategy. The benchmark infrastructure is the same — just swap the corpus.

5. **Q:** Why does your benchmark reuse the M0 gold set?
   **A:** Comparability. The M0 baseline gives recall@1=0.800 with the overlapping chunker. The M2 benchmark shows overlapping at 0.900 (better corpus, no PowerShell artifacts). If you used a different gold set, the numbers wouldn't be directly comparable. Reusing the gold set makes changes in recall attributable to chunker choice, not gold set variation.

6. **Q:** Semantic chunking uses more compute (2x embeddings) and ranked last. Under what conditions would you still use it?
   **A:** Long-form narrative content where adjacent paragraphs form a coherent arc — meeting notes, research papers, novels, blog articles. The question is always "do adjacent paragraphs belong together semantically?" For our RAG primer (each paragraph = one discrete concept), no. For a 40-page research paper's literature review (flowing argument across 20 paragraphs), yes.

7. **Q:** What's the right way to clean corpus data before benchmarking?
   **A:** Programmatic construction (`make_corpus.py`) not shell one-liners. Shell commands (PowerShell Set-Content, bash echo) inject artifacts — command strings, BOM markers, encoding inconsistencies. Python strings are clean, cross-platform, version-controllable. The corpus creation script is part of the benchmark, not infrastructure around it.
